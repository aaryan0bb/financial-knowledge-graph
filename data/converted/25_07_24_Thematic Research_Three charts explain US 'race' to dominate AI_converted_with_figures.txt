"Race to achieve global dominance" is a zero-sum game                     Research Analyst

The US will do "whatever it takes to lead the world in Al," President Donald Trump 
said yesterday at an event to launch a 23-page White House Al Action Plan 
reinforced by three executive orders. 

America is in a "race to achieve global dominance in Al," the paper says. It presents 
Al as "an industrial revolution, an information revolution, and a renaissance - all at 
once", but also as a zero-sum game that may "reshape the global balance of 
power". 

The US needs to dismantle regulation that might hinder innovation, build out its 
infrastructure and energy capacity, and ensure that allies use cutting-edge 
American technology while rivals - namely China - face tighter export controls, 
according to the plan. 

One of Trump's first acts on taking office in January was to tear up the Biden 
Administration's Executive Order on Al from October 2023, with its title underling 
its focus on the "Safe, Secure and Trustworthy Development and Use of Al", and to 
issue his own, entitled "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Al". 

Three charts explain why this is such a priority for this administration: 

1.Innovation: power to companies 
The US is clearly the world's leading force in Al. It plays host to 42 of the top 50 Al 
companies, 33 of them in California, according to Forbes. US companies pocketed 
almost three quarters of the private equity and venture capital investments last year, 
data from the Stanford Al Index show. (The minimum investment in the study was 
$1.5m.) Forty of the most notable Al models last year came from the US, compared 
with 15 from China and just three from Europe. 

The US has not taken the aggressive regulatory approach of the European Union, 
whose comprehensive Al Act is currently rolling out amid an ongoing debate about 
how it should be implemented.

The tide has turned, with Silicon Valley leaders taking high-profile roles in the 
administration, including two of the three signatories to the Action Plan: Michael 
Kratsios, White House Office of Science and Technology Policy Director, and a 
former venture capitalist and protégé of PayPal founder Peter Thiel; and David 
Sacks, Al and crypto czar, and a leading technology investor now known as one of 
the hosts of the All-In podcast. 

There has also been a surge in lobbying on Al, with more than 500 companies 
seeking to influence the White House and Congress between January and June, the 
Financial Times reported yesterday, citing new federal disclosures. 

The Action Plan puts cutting red tape front and centre in accelerating Al innovation, 
saying the federal government should withhold funding from states with 
burdensome Al regulations and urging several agencies to evaluate and repeal 
policies that impede innovation. 

It also recommends that the Federal Trade Commission review investigations and 
orders to ensure that they do not "unduly burden Al innovation". That could ease the 
pressure on companies such as Microsoft, which is subject to a lengthy antitrust 
probe. President Trump said yesterday that he had contemplated breaking up 
Nvidia, the $4trn Al chip producer, before discovering that would be "very hard".

The plan was broadly welcomed by the tech industry, prompting some critics to 
brand it a sell-out, but there is a perhaps surprising underlying emphasis on risk 
management throughout the plan, ranging from interpretability to cybersecurity. 

2.Infrastructure: powering up 
Romantic notions of Al existing in a cloud are far from reality. The technology 
depends on energy-hungry data centres, around half of which are in the US. 

The Action Plan calls for streamlined permitting, underlined by an executive order 
also announced yesterday; there's no point in Alphabet's Google pledging to make 
$85bn in capital expenditure this year, as it did yesterday, if it can't get the permits 
to build the data centres or the power to run them. The plan also makes the case for 
a revitalised domestic semiconductor-manufacturing sector; and greater security 
both for corporate and military and intelligence users. 

Much of the focus though is on energy. Power usage by data centres in the US more 
than doubled from less than 2 percent in 2018 to 4.4 percent in 2023, and is set to 
rise to as much as 12 percent, or 580 TWh in 2028, according to the US Department 
of Energy's Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory.

One leading startup, Anthropic, said in a recent white paper that the US Al sector 
will need at least 50GW of electric capacity by 2028, with 5GW data centres needed 
to train the most advanced models. 

Other countries, such as Ireland, have seen data centres consuming more than 20 
percent of the country's electricity; the US does not want to follow. After two 
decades of stagnant demand, strain is being put on the system not just by growth 
in data centres but even more so by a surge in industrial use and electric transport. 

There has already been some progress. Investments in the electricity grid have 
grown worldwide after generally falling in the late 2010s, but since 2020 they have 
taken off again - and nowhere more so than in North America.

 A total of $114bn was invested in the grid there last year, up from the mid-$70bns 
 each year in the latter half of the 2010s, according to the International Energy 
 Agency. China invested $89bn in its grid last year and Europe $85bn, the data show. 

 Now the US Action Plan foresees preventing the premature decommissioning of 
 critical power generation resources; upgrading existing transmission systems and 
 power lines to increase the amount of energy that can be transmitted; and 
 encouraging the expansion of the grid with new technologies such as enhanced 
geothermal, nuclear fission and nuclear fusion. 

 3.International Al: projecting power 
Just because the US is ahead of rivals today does not mean it will be tomorrow. 

 The final portion of the Action Plan focuses on national security - emphasised by the 
 fact that the third signatory to the Action Plan was Secretary of State Marco Rubio. 
 It stresses that the US should lead an "enduring global alliance, while preventing 
 our adversaries from free-riding on our innovation and investment". 

The launch of a cutting-edge Al model by China's DeepSeek in January caused a 
rout in US tech stocks, including an 18 percent drop in the market cap of Nvidia. The 
 initial focus was on how the model had apparently been developed for a fraction of 
 the cost of comparable US models, potentially calling into question several hundred 
 billion dollars of investment in the most advanced Al chips at home. 

 But the more troubling aspect from a US national security point of view was that 
 either the tight controls on exports of those chips and other Al components to China 
 were not effective - or that they were effective - but that Chinese inventiveness with 
older chips or home-grown equivalents meant it didn't matter. 

As the chart shows, Chinese models are rapidly approaching the capabilities of 
 those from the US. The performance gap on major benchmarks is closing, with the 
 top US models scoring 1,385 in LMSYS Chatbot Arena as of February, barely ahead 
 of the 1,362 scored by their Chinese counterparts.

China also dominates in Al patents, accounting for 70 percent of grants in 2023, 
trouncing the US with just 14 percent, according to the Stanford Al Index. Whatever 
the respective quality of those patents, US policymakers clearly do not want to 
appear complacent. 

The Action Plan looks to put the weight of the Department of Commerce towards 
selling Al packages to allies, codified in another new executive order on "promoting 
the export of the American Al technology stack". It also pledges to invest in areas 
such as biosecurity. 

However, most of the emphasis is on throwing obstacles in the way of China's use 
of Al to gain a competitive edge. 

That includes using location verification features on advanced Al compute to 
tighten export control enforcement; developing new controls to plug loopholes, 
such as exports of component sub-systems; and compelling allies to ensure they 
reinforce export controls and imposing penalties such as tariffs on them if they do 
not. 

It remains to be seen how this will work in practice in the face of Realpolitik: last 
week the administration said it would relax export controls on Nvidia and AMD 
selling some (less sophisticated) Al chips to China in return for China restarting its 
exports of some rare earths to the US. 

Some underlying reassurance: jobs, ideology and safety 
The Action Plan also lays out some overarching principles in an attempt to reassure 
a sceptical public. A global Ipsos poll last year showed that Americans are among 
the least enthusiastic citizens worldwide about Al, with fewer than 40 percent 
agreeing that it has more benefits than drawbacks. 

The plan tells American workers that Al will enhance their lives by complementing 
their work, not replacing it. Al systems must be trustworthy and "free from 
ideological bias", it says - accompanied by a third new executive order called 
"preventing woke Al in the federal government" to prevent agency heads from 
procuring Al models that fail to meet its standards for truth and neutrality. And there 
will be "constant vigilance" of misuse, theft or "emerging and unforeseen risks".
