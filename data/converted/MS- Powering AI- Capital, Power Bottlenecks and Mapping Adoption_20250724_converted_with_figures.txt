What are investors asking? 

                     How will GPUs be financed, particularly for non-IG buyers? 
                     While it is clear that hyperscalers have both the cash on hand and access to capital markets necessary to 
                     buy GPUs and build/lease data centers as needed, investors are more skeptical about non-IG and relatively 
                     new Al players' ability to finance their purchases of GPUs in size and over time. Loans backed by GPUs that 
                     amortize over time (and over a shorter period than the chips' useful life) is likely to be a popular solution. 
                     Moreover, companies that lease these GPUs to high-quality counterparties benefit from strong contractual 
                     terms that ensure cash flow over the term of the loan. We think public securitization could be a potential 
                     solution, but not likely until later this decade. HY unsecured issuance is also an option but will come at a cost 
                     to the borrower with rates in the high-single digits to mid-teens. 

                     Is this another credit bubble? Why is this time different? 
                     Seasoned credit investors will recall that through the later part of the 90s, corporates were very aggressive 
                     with their capital allocation, and debt/leverage increased sharply. The period ultimately ended with the 
                     sharpest default cycle in recent history from 2000-04. Clearly, debt growth of this magnitude runs into the 
                     risk of obsolescence, slower monetization, and weak macro conditions. An encouraging sign, though, is the 
                     diverse pools of capital that are available today, which can distribute the warehousing of credit risk (unlike 
                     the late 1990s when it was concentrated on corporate balance sheets). The ultra-high-quality credit profile of 
                     hyperscalers and significant cash on hand mean less sensitivity to macro conditions.

   Definition of a "de-bottlenecking" solution: any product that can result in data centers powering up more 
   rapidly than the default approach (file a grid interconnection request at a greenfield site) 

   Option #1: build power plants at the data center site, and go off-grid while waiting for grid

   - Favors natural gas and coal power, but we could also see renewables, nuclear power and energy storage 

   - Significant potential operational issues with not being connected to the grid

   - Increasingly facing political and grid operator pushback; tends to result in higher power prices 

   Option #3: redirect power available to a Bitcoin site or industrial power user 

   - We view this as an attractive option, but not large enough to resolve the bottleneck issue

  Anthropic: "all else equal, scaling up the training of Al systems leads to smoothly better results on a range 
  of cognitive tasks, across the board ... These differences tend to have huge implications in practice - another 
 factor of 10 may correspond to the difference between an undergraduate and PhD skill level - and thus 
  companies are investing heavily in training these models ... The field is constantly coming up with ideas, large 
  and small, that make things more effective or efficient: it could be an improvement to the architecture of the 
  model ... or simply a way of running the model more efficiently on the underlying hardware. New generations 
  of hardware also have the same effect. What    this typically does is shift the curve: if the innovation is a 2x 
  'compute multiplier' (CM), then it allows you to get 40% on a coding task for $5M instead of $10M; or 60% for 
  $50M instead of $100M, etc ... Because the value of having a more intelligent system is so high, this 
  shifting of the curve typically causes companies to spend more, not less, on training models: the 
  gains in cost efficiency end up entirely devoted to training smarter models, limited only by the 
  company's financial resources. People are naturally attracted to the idea that 'first something is expensive, 
 then it gets cheaper' - as if Al is a single thing of constant quality, and when it gets cheaper, we'll use fewer 
  chips to train it. But what's important is the scaling curve: when it shifts, we simply traverse it faster, because 
  the value of what's at the end of the curve is so high."
