US: higher tariffs, greater impacts 
We view the recently-announced 15% tariff on Japan and 19% tariff on the 
Philippines and Indonesia as consistent with our expectation that tariffs are moving 
higher, with the US effective tariff rate likely to rise by a total 17pp by 2027. We 
estimate this will boost US core PCE prices by around 1.7% cumulatively over the 
next couple of years (partly reflecting a slightly smaller pass-through of higher costs 
to consumer prices than we previously assumed) and, as such, now expect core 
PCE inflation of 3.3%/2.7%/2.4% yoy in December 2025/2026/2027 (vs. 
3.4%/2.6%/2.0% previously). We also expect higher tariffs to lower GDP growth by 
around 1 pp/0.4pp/0.3pp yoy over each of the next three years as a smaller trade 
deficit partly offsets the growth drag from lower consumption and investment. All 
that said, we see risks to our baseline tariff expectations as skewed slightly to the 
upside, which suggests modest upside risk to our inflation forecasts and downside 
risk to our growth forecasts through 2027.

                          Central banks: at different easing stages 
                          We expect unchanged rates and limited shifts in communication at tomorrow's ECB 
                          meeting given that the incoming Euro area macro data have been broadly consistent 
                          with the June staff projections. We also expect President Lagarde to reiterate that policy 
                          is in a good place, but that the ECB stands ready to respond to changes in the economic 
                          outlook on the back of trade policy developments. While we see downside risks to our 
                          Euro area growth forecasts from higher US tariffs for the remainder of this year, we 
                          remain optimistic on Euro area growth in 2026 owing to a cyclical pickup in Germany on 
                          the back of its fiscal expansion and continued strength in Spain. As such, we believe 
                          that the ECB easing cycle is probably nearing its end, and continue to expect one last 
                          rate cut to 1.75% in September. 

                          While the end of ECB cuts is in sight, a return to Fed cuts is now coming into view. The 
                          slowdown in US growth in 1H25 and rising risk of the labor market hitting "stall speed" 
                          has strengthened the case for earlier easing, with both Governor Waller and Vice Chair 
                          for Supervision Bowman likely to vote in favor of cutting rates at next week's FOMC 
                          meeting. But we believe that other committee members will want to wait for 
                          confirmation that the tariff impact really is a one-time price level shock and/or for 
                          evidence of more labor market softening. As such, we continue to expect the Fed to 
                          next cut in September, with two more 25bp cuts later this year and two 25bp cuts next 
                          year likely to follow, for a terminal rate of 3-3.25%. This return to Fed easing should push 
                          down Treasury yields and the Dollar while boosting global equities and gold. That said, 
                          threats to Fed independence that result in a further rise in long-term inflation 
                          expectations could introduce greater uncertainty into the Fed's reaction function and 
                          cloud the policy outlook.

                          What else is on our radar? 
                              We're focused on the US housing outlook amid a weak spring selling season, 
                              faltering residential investment on the back of a rebound in mortgage rates, and 
                              emerging difficulties absorbing robust housing supply. We expect residential 
                              investment growth to remain weak for the remainder of the year and forecast that 
                              national home prices will rise just 0.2% this year and 0.8% next year 
                              (December-over-December). 

                              We're also keeping an eye on US net immigration, which we estimate declined from 
                              an annualized pace of 0.6mn in April to 0.5mn in June, which aligns with our 
                             expectation that immigration will stabilize at around 0.5mn annually (vs. the 
                              pre-pandemic trend of 1.0mn). We expect this gradual slowdown to lower the 
                              breakeven rate-the rate of payroll job growth needed to keep the unemployment 
                              rate stable-to 70k per month by end-2025 from an estimated 90k currently. 

                              Finally, we're watching Japanese politics following the recent Upper House elections 
                              that resulted in a narrow loss of the ruling coalition's (Liberal Democratic Party + 
                              Komeito) majority. We think a minority government of the current ruling coalition will 
                              continue, although cooperation with opposition parties will likely become even more 
                              crucial for passing legislation. We also believe that the opposition parties' focus on 
                              proposals to cut consumption taxes and increase social security measures portends 
                              an expansionary bias in fiscal policy ahead.
